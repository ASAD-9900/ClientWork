Hospitality Architecture & Restaurant Design

Mediterranean Character with a Neighbourhood Feel

This Notting Hill restaurant scheme takes its direction from warm colours, curved architectural forms and contrasting textures.

Arched openings create a sequence of dining settings, giving the interior a sense of progression. Terracotta tones establish warmth, while olive seating, timber furniture and pale table surfaces introduce balance.

The hearth-cooking concept provides a potential focal point for the guest experience. Its successful integration depends on coordinating the dining layout with the cooking equipment, specialist services and operational requirements.

Commissioned within Kensington and Chelsea and serving clients across nearby Knightsbridge, this project reflects our studio's architectural approach to hospitality restaurant interior design.

The Design Brief

The supplied brief centres on creating a sociable Mediterranean dining environment with a series of smaller settings rather than one undifferentiated room.

  • Establish a distinctive identity through arches, colour and texture.
  • Provide comfortable seating for different party sizes.
  • Connect guests visually with the cooking concept where appropriate.
  • Preserve clear circulation between dining areas.
  • Select finishes suitable for hospitality use.
  • Create a relaxed atmosphere through coordinated lighting.
Design Problem Solving

Design Challenges & Architectural Responses

Every project is shaped by physical site parameters, technical regulations, and tailored spatial solutions. Detailed architectural coordination was resolved prior to site commencement using precise 3D CGI render services.

01 Challenge

Creating Intimacy without Restricting Movement

Architectural divisions can give a restaurant character, but they must also support clear routes through the venue.

Design Response

Coordinate archways, tables and service routes together. Check sightlines and access between the different dining settings before confirming capacity.

02 Challenge

Integrating Hearth Cooking with the Dining Room

Wood-fired or charcoal cooking introduces specialist requirements beyond the decorative treatment of the kitchen.

Design Response

Establish the equipment and fuel choices early, with appropriate specialist assessment of extraction, ventilation, fire precautions and the premises. The interior design should develop around the agreed technical solution.

03 Challenge

Counter seating near cooking equipment must be assessed for its position, comfort and suitability.

Balancing Banquettes with Flexible Seating Fixed booths can define intimate settings, while movable tables help accommodate different groups.

Design Response

Develop seat dimensions, table positions and circulation together. Confirm practical group sizes from the measured layout rather than assuming every booth can seat four to six guests.

04 Challenge

Selecting Textured Finishes for Commercial Use

Terracotta, plaster, timber and terrazzo create character but have different maintenance requirements.

Design Response

Review the exact products, finishes and supplier guidance for each application. Assess flooring for expected conditions, counters for their intended use and table surfaces for cleaning and repair.

05 Challenge

Keeping Conversation Comfortable

Hard floors and curved plaster surfaces can contribute to sound reflections.

Design Response

Consider room acoustics alongside ceilings, walls and furnishings. Upholstery can contribute softness, but acoustic performance should be assessed rather than inferred from appearance.

06 Challenge

Combining Atmosphere with Practical Lighting

Low-level lighting should create warmth while allowing guests and staff to use the space comfortably.

Design Response

Combine decorative wall fittings with table, circulation and task lighting. Test the proposed settings during commissioning.

These are design considerations relevant to the scheme. Actual site problems, client revisions and installed technical solutions require confirmation from project records.

01 Investment Guide

What Would a Similar Restaurant Fit-Out Cost?

£200,000–£450,000 excluding VAT.

This is an initial editorial estimate for a defined interior fit-out involving architectural finishes, selected partitions or decorative arches, fitted seating, joinery, furniture, lighting and installation.

  • Design and technical coordination: Surveys, layouts, drawings and specifications
  • Interior fit-out: Flooring, ceilings, decoration and agreed architectural features
  • Fitted seating and joinery: Banquettes, counters, storage and service stations
  • Furniture: Dining tables, chairs and counter seating
  • Lighting and electrical work: Fittings, controls and agreed alterations
  • Specialist coordination: Interfaces with kitchen, ventilation and other services
  • Installation and handover: Delivery, fitting, commissioning coordination and snagging
  • Wood-fired ovens, charcoal grills, specialist extraction, fire-protection systems and major services upgrades require separate quotations unless expressly included.: Property acquisition, rent deposits, licensing costs, operating stock and pre-opening staffing are excluded. Professional fees and approval-related charges should be identified separately.

Allow a separate contingency of 10–15%, reviewed following survey, with VAT added where applicable.

02 Delivery Schedule

How Long Would a Similar Project Take?

6–12 months

For initial planning, allow approximately 6–12 months, with some stages overlapping.

  • Brief, surveys and feasibility: 3–5 weeks
  • Layout planning and concept design: 4–6 weeks
  • Detailed design and specialist coordination: 6–10 weeks
  • Approvals and landlord review: Confirm for the premises and proposed cooking methods
  • Procurement and manufacture: 8–16 weeks or longer for selected items
  • Site fit-out and installation: 10–18 weeks
  • Commissioning, snagging and handover: 2–3 weeks
  • Hearth-cooking requirements, extraction feasibility, approvals and specialist equipment deliveries may extend the programme.: The opening date should also allow for commissioning and operational preparations.
