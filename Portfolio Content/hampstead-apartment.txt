Making More of a Compact London Home
This Hampstead apartment design focuses on how a smaller home can accommodate relaxing, dining, working and storage within a coordinated interior.
Pale timber tones, simple cabinetry and restrained finishes create a consistent design direction. Concealed cupboards reduce visual clutter, while a built-in dining bench makes use of the room’s perimeter.
The approach gives practical decisions—such as cupboard openings, furniture dimensions and routes between rooms—as much attention as colour and styling.

The Design Brief
The supplied brief centres on making better use of the apartment’s available space without filling it with separate pieces of furniture.
The principal objectives are to:
Improve movement between the main living spaces.
Introduce storage for household belongings and equipment.
Accommodate dining without dominating the living area.
Provide a discreet place for occasional home working.
Create a coordinated kitchen and shower room.
Maintain a calm, uncluttered appearance.
Design Challenges and Responses
Accommodating Several Activities within a Small Footprint
Living, dining and working arrangements can compete for the same floor space.
Design response: Develop the furniture layout and storage together. Position larger elements around the room’s perimeter where appropriate, preserving usable central space and clear routes between rooms.
The benefit is a layout that supports daily routines without relying on moving furniture whenever the room changes function.
Introducing Storage without Overwhelming the Interior
Multiple freestanding cupboards can make a compact apartment feel fragmented.
Design response: Coordinate fitted storage into a consistent run of cabinetry, combining closed cupboards with selected display shelving. Internal arrangements should respond to the items being stored rather than appearance alone.
An integrated desk can provide a work setting that closes away when it is no longer needed.
Making Room for Dining
A conventional dining arrangement requires space for the table, chairs and movement around them.
Design response: Use a built-in banquette where the room’s dimensions support it. Under-seat storage adds another function, while the table and remaining chairs can be selected around the available clearance.
Seating capacity should be confirmed from the measured layout before being advertised.
Keeping Circulation Areas Useful and Uncluttered
Corridors can become congested when storage projects into walking routes.
Design response: Check cupboard depths, door swings and access requirements together. Coordinate finishes between the hallway and adjacent rooms to create visual continuity.
Planning a Practical Shower Room
A compact bathroom requires careful coordination of sanitaryware, storage and access.
Design response: Establish the shower enclosure, basin position and usable clearances before selecting finishes. Detailed design should also address ventilation, waterproofing, maintenance and access to concealed components.
These describe the scheme’s design considerations. Actual structural alterations, site discoveries and client-requested changes require confirmation from project records.
Key Design Features
Concealed Storage
Flush cabinetry provides a consistent backdrop, with space allocated for everyday belongings, media equipment and household storage.
Built-In Dining Seating
An upholstered banquette creates a defined dining nook while incorporating useful storage beneath the seat.
Coordinated Media Shelving
A combination of open shelving and closed cupboards allows selected objects to be displayed while keeping equipment and cables less prominent.
Flexible Lighting
Task lighting supports cooking and working, while softer ambient lighting creates a more relaxed evening setting. Fitting positions should be coordinated with cabinetry and the existing ceiling construction.

Managing Design Changes
In a compact apartment, even a small dimensional change can affect neighbouring furniture or cabinetry.
Layouts, appliance specifications and joinery drawings should therefore be coordinated and approved before manufacture. Later changes should be recorded with their cost and programme implications before work proceeds.
